This is the last of four posts about the *Backrooms* film. [Part 1](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-1-the-degrading-fractal/) was the first night: everything it reminded me of, and the degrading fractal underneath. [Part 2](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-2-the-one-thing-it-cant-do/) followed that pattern down into the Abyss and through Clark's story, up to the moment the pirate copy of him takes him. [Part 3](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-3-every-place-that-ever-was/) was about the place itself and Async, the research institute watching it. This one is Mary's, and after Part 3's jumping around, it follows her in order up to the end of the film, then steps back to what it all means. Same as before: the ideas are mine unless I say they're Claude's, and there are spoilers for the whole film.

If you're starting here, the short version: Clark runs a failing furniture store in 1990, and the way into the Backrooms is through its basement. He takes Kat, his assistant manager, and her boyfriend Bobby in with him, and Bobby gets killed down there. The place is full of still lifes, slow, hollow copies of people, and one far worse copy, a pirate version of Clark based on the mascot from his store's TV commercials. Mary Kline, Clark's therapist, follows him in, and he takes her prisoner at a dinner table surrounded by still lifes, letting her go only once he's decided to stay, right before the pirate comes for him. Async, the research institute that was down there before any of them, is watching all of it, and Phil is the Async scientist who interviews Mary at the end.

## Before she goes in

The windows go back to Mary's childhood. Her mother, Nora, seems to have been agoraphobic and was eventually institutionalized, and when young Mary reaches to open a window, Nora stops her. As [greg_nog points out](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GDnjrXEpaFY), that's probably where the language in her book, *The Window Within*, comes from. The book is about people stuck in the same patterns, and the film spells out what the window means. Early on, Clark drives out one night and sits in his car outside the house he's been thrown out of, listening to what seems to be the book's companion audio program. On the tape Mary says the mind builds walls to protect itself, until you're left watching your life from behind a single window. She can help you open it, she says, because "the window isn't locked".

There's a scene of her alone on her couch, watching the TV ad for the book, which mirrors Clark watching TV from the display bed he sleeps in at his store: two people living alone, and maybe lonely. Clark uses her words, too. In the voice-over just before she wakes up at his dinner table, he's reciting her lines about loops and habits almost word for word, and Claude pointed out that the message he leaves her, saying he's opened the window and won't be coming back, does the same thing with her book, as a way of saying goodbye. The girl who wasn't allowed to open a window grew up to write a book about opening them, and then a patient used the phrase to tell her he was never coming back.

## For her own good

That message wasn't the first sign. Early on, in her office, before he tells her anything about the Backrooms, Clark asks Mary what the criteria are for a 5150, the code for an involuntary psychiatric hold, and she answers straight: being a danger to yourself or someone else. Clark knew how he was going to sound. For a woman whose mother was committed, I'd guess it isn't an abstract question either. When she does go looking for him, the opening in his basement wall is already outlined in blue painter's tape, but she doesn't go in until a buzzing fly vanishes into the wall right in front of her.

In Part 2 I asked how Clark's sleeper hold could be for her own good. The film's answer is where it cuts next: straight from her passing out to Mary as a child, visiting the institution where her mother was committed, which is where "for your own good" comes from in her life. She grew up to be a therapist, and I'd guess for the reason a lot of people do: to become someone who helps, instead of someone who gets hurt. Now one of her own patients has tied her to a chair.

There's a detail in that cut I only caught on a rewatch. The institution has soft jazz playing, with a bit of an elevator-music feel, and it reminded me of a piece of music called "Still Life" that the Backrooms plays out of its walls. They aren't the same piece, but something with that same soothing, canned feel was playing in the place where Mary's mother was locked away. She'll hear the Backrooms' own version on her way out.

## Getting out

With Clark gone, taken by the pirate right after he let her go, Mary still has to get out. She runs from the dining room straight into a street, with houses, garage doors, and a stop sign, all under a drop ceiling and fluorescent lights. At the far end is a locked door, and behind her, at a distance, the pirate is following, dragging Clark's body, which it drops in the middle of the street. She turns to a door on the left, bumps her head on more wind chimes, and comes to a room full of couches, where she hurts her foot.

She's limping by the time she crawls through the cat flap of a locked door into the room with the bottomless pit from Part 1 and edges along a narrow ledge around the walls (that room is a whole OSHA violation). The only way forward is a short flight of stairs up to a small square door, like the little door at the top of the climb in the throne room from Part 1, except that one had three knobs and only one of them worked, and this one has a single knob. It falls off into the pit when she opens it. This room is where "Still Life" comes back, playing out of the walls, and the captions call it "eerie dreamy music overlapping sinister music".

Behind her the pirate is tearing through the locked door as she pulls herself up through a hole in the ceiling into the basement of Clark's store. Only on the main floor does she realize it's another copy. There's even an "EVERYTHING MUST GO!" sale sign up, the same words as the title of the extra footage after the credits. She passes another of Async's cardboard cutouts, this one ringed with gas canisters, heads for the entrance, and finds the doors open onto nothing but a beige wall, like the rest of the Backrooms. Async has a camera in there. When the pirate catches up, she smashes its peg leg with a big hat rack (her limp seems to vanish for the fight, either an oversight or adrenaline), and as she runs she trips the gas trap, which catches them both.

She ends up on the floor, disoriented, and when she turns around the pirate is almost on top of her, trying to eat her (out of hunger, I think, more than cruelty). Then she remembers what's in her pocket and pulls it out: a piece of concrete from the house she grew up in, with her own handprint in it from when she was a child. She hits the pirate in the face again and again, until the concrete breaks, and the sound is awful, wet, and squelching, while the pirate's face gets badly messed up. It looks and sounds like flesh, which the more dormant still lifes, the ones that are basically furniture, don't seem to be.

Claude pointed out that she gets free by smashing a piece of her own childhood, and her childhood, with her mother and the windows she wasn't allowed to open, is exactly what the Backrooms had been copying all around her. The house she grew up in was number 628, and a friend encouraged me to look up its gematria, where each Hebrew letter has a number value: 628 spells ששכח, roughly "the one who forgot", and also רק חשך, "only darkness". Claude's reading was that the world forgot that house when it was torn down, Mary never could, and the Backrooms copies it forever.

The way out made me even surer of what I said in Part 2, that the pirate isn't malicious. She squeezes through a narrow passage that runs from the copy of Clark's store to a room on the other side, and to me it looks almost like a birth canal. As she looks back, the pirate lets out a howl that sounds more like grief, as if it's sad she's leaving. Then she comes out the other end into a room full of Async people in hazmat suits, who seem completely freaked out to see her and immediately spray her with gas. It's not much of a welcome into the world.

## The interview

On the first night I noticed that Mary asks to leave twice, once tied up at the dinner table and once at Async's interview table at the very end, with no say either time. Claude took that further. Mary keeps ending up at a table where one side sets the terms: in her own therapy office that's her, then it's Clark at his dinner table, and finally Async in the interview room. The same table keeps coming back around, with her in a different seat each time. And it starts before any table. In that institution scene, young Mary just stands and watches as her mother, sedated but wild-eyed, is wheeled away in her wheelchair, with no more say than she has at either table.

The third table is the interview with Phil. She came out of the Backrooms bloodied and in her own clothes, and in the interview she's cleaned up and changed, so some unknown amount of time has passed. On the walk to it, she passes a window onto a room with dozens more of those cardboard cutouts from Part 3, so for Async the bait looks like an industrial operation. Somewhere along that walk there's also a brief glimpse of the pirate itself, tied down and being dissected. One way or another, the bait got them what they were after.

The interview room has little windows high on the wall, too small and too high to open, and you can hear birds outside. Birds keep turning up all through this film: seagulls fly over a homeless person with a cart of bottles just before Mary parks at Clark's store, and Part 3 has the one that crashes into a wall. Here I wonder if they mean her freedom is behind a window again.

The ad for her book opens by asking if you ever feel like you're living "behind glass", and now she literally is. You can hear the chimes from Part 3 in here, too. Someone online pointed out the paint as well: the lower half of the walls is yellow and the upper half white, and the shot frames her right across the line. Given the director Kane Parsons's track record, it may well be on purpose, the yellow standing for the Backrooms.

On a rewatch, what stood out to me about that room was consent. Phil asks if she minds answering some questions, she doesn't say anything, and he carries on anyway: the form of consent with none of the substance. Part 2 had the one clean bit of consent in the film, at the dinner table, and this is its opposite. He holds up a photo of Clark's store and has her confirm what he already knows, and when she mentions Clark, he shows her the badly redacted photo from Part 2, of Clark facing the pirate. Claude added that it's as if he's checking whether her memory is still a faithful copy, and that Async has built the Backrooms' trap without any help from the Backrooms.

Phil does most of the talking, and she stays mostly quiet, except to ask once or twice when she'll be allowed to leave. That decision, he tells her, isn't up to him, which is almost exactly what she told Clark at the table when he asked her how to fix himself. Once she realizes there's no way out, she seems to start dissociating. There's a one-way mirror on one wall, and there seem to be people behind it watching her that she can't see. At one point Phil glances at it as if he's checking whether he's allowed to say something. It's the one-way wall from Part 1 again, this time built on purpose. It's the last of her tables, and the worst one, because nobody in it is degraded at all.

## The ending

After the interview, the film ends on a montage of places, most of them from her life: the house she grew up in, which has since been demolished, her book on display in a bookstore, the house where she lives and sees clients now, and her bedroom. Then come places that aren't hers: missing posters for Kat and Bobby on telephone poles down there, the grassy yard outside their place, and a degraded copy of the parking lot outside Clark's store. I think they're there to show how much the place has copied, and the posters hint that time has passed. Last is a badly copied version of the interview room with Mary sitting in it.

The first time, I wondered whether she'd imagined the whole thing. Now I think the ending is less ambiguous than I gave it credit for, because the Mary down there is visibly a still life. Her face is all messed up, and something is very clearly off about her hands, as if she has too many fingers (funnily enough, hands are the thing AI image generators have historically struggled to get right).

The occult tradition from Part 2 even has a name for that last shot, which Claude brought up. Someone who reaches the Abyss and won't let go of their self doesn't get destroyed. They get preserved, sealed inside a shell of who they were, and Crowley's system calls that a Black Brother. A copy of Mary, sitting forever in a room that's remembered wrong, possibly without knowing what she is, is about as close to that as a picture gets.

## Coming back

Back to the dinner table, though. [One of the analysis videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A6adyYDMpD8) I watched afterwards read Mary's moment there, when she tells Clark she can't save anyone, as bravery. I think it's bravery and defeat at once. She's at her lowest point in the film when she realizes she can't save anyone, and that same realization is what frees her. Maybe she looks back on it later and is half grateful for it, even though it came from being kidnapped and tied up by her own patient. I honestly don't know how she goes back to doing therapy after that, though Async probably isn't going to let her go anyway, and my guess is she ends up working for them.

The YouTube series shows how badly that can go for someone who comes back out. Peter Tench was an Async researcher who vanished on an expedition, and since Async couldn't tell the public what had happened, it [invented a death for him](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j6klSI8GlXI), and his family [held a funeral](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ye6YpxFE9jk). Two months later he walked back out, and for him only a few hours had passed.

Async kept him in a secure room while it worked out how to bring a legally dead man back, and the waiting wore him down. By Async's own account he came to believe he was still inside an illusion the place had made, and that the people around him meant him harm. Claude's point was that once they'd faked his death, that wasn't such a crazy conclusion. He broke out, forced his way back into the Backrooms, and later shot a researcher before fleeing to the surface, where, Async says, he was found dead on a hillside after falling onto a rock. But the footage of him running to that hillside is never shown, and the video's last glitchy frames show a man, presumably him, running near a highway, so Async may not be telling the truth about that either.

That's a person coming back out. It made me wonder about the reverse, too. What if a still life got out into the real world? Would it even still be alive, or would it slowly morph into something closer to the real thing? I haven't seen anyone explore that.

## Still there

In Part 1 I said I came out of the film wishing there was more, and in a way there is. [The YouTube series](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLVAh-MgDVqvDUEq6qDXqORBioE4Yhol_z) has hours more of it (I've since watched it twice through). Other people's Backrooms videos add their own strangeness, like a cameraman who steps out into a forest or a blizzard and then wanders back in, or a man who gets out only to see himself and his friend in the distance, going in, in the past. The fan analysis seems endless, and every answer the film gives opens a few more questions. Kane Parsons very much knows what he's doing, and the attention to detail is impressive: almost everything on screen connects to something else on screen.

"Still Life", the music from the walls, is a good example. Parsons wrote it himself, and it's on [the third volume of his score](https://kanepixels.bandcamp.com/album/backrooms-original-score-vol-3) for the YouTube series. In the film it comes out of speakers on the walls in parts of the Backrooms: the dark lower level where Bobby gets killed, the stretch Clark runs through after, and the pit room Mary edges around with the pirate behind her.

My first rule for it was that something bad happens when it plays. Then I thought it announced a still life. Claude took it one step further: it's a habitat marker, playing wherever the still lifes live, and the pirate is the worst of them. Even the captions can't settle on what it is: "relaxing music playing loudly" where Bobby dies, and something "eerie" and "sinister" in the pit room.

There's always another thread like that to pull. In Part 1 I called the Backrooms a degrading fractal, one that gets worse the closer you zoom in, and I've kept zooming in anyway. I said then that I'd watched it somewhere around eight times. I still can't tell you the real number, which is about the most Backrooms thing my memory could do. I'm aware of how that sounds.

That's the film I couldn't stop rewatching.
