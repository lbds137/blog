When I [picked this blog back up](/blog/posts/picking-this-back-up/), I said most new posts would be written with a lot of help from AI, specifically Claude, and that this only works if what goes up still sounds like me. My four posts on the [Backrooms film](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-1-the-degrading-fractal/) were the first real test of that. Now that the series is done, Claude and I went back and checked how it went, and the short version is that the ideas were mostly mine, but the sentences were mostly Claude's. So, in the interest of transparency and disclosure, this is how the sausage is made. In other contexts, that might turn you off from ever eating sausage again, but I just think it's interesting. In a world of non-disclosed AI slop everywhere, I think it's refreshing to have somebody who uses AI be transparent about their ideas and about how they use AI to convey those ideas.

## How a post gets made

I talk a subject through with Claude first, often by dictating long voice messages (my side of the Backrooms conversations came to 199 messages). Claude collects my messages into one file and drafts the post from them, following a voice guide, which is a file that describes how I write. Then I read the draft and push back until I'm happy with it. Nothing goes up without my OK. Where an idea is Claude's, the post says so. It should be a collaboration that's honest rather than us not including good ideas just because they came from Claude.

After Part 4 went up, a fresh copy of Claude that hadn't worked on the drafts read my old posts, my messages, and the four Backrooms posts, and compared them. Its verdict was that the ideas were right and the voice was half right. At the sentence level, the posts were Claude's prose carrying my observations. Then we put a number on it with a small script. Of the 544 sentences in the series, 60 share a run of five or more words with something I said, which comes to 11%. (For comparison, an essay about the film that Claude wrote in its own words scores about 3%.)

## What 11% means

Now, 11% of my wording does not mean 11% of my ideas. The whole point of this is to figure out how best to communicate my ideas, and that doesn't necessarily mean copying verbatim what I said. So yes, it is a strong admission to make in public, but also it's really not the whole picture. My ideas are more important than the specific wording that is used in conveying them. Obviously we can do better than 11%, and I do think quantifying it like that will make it easier to make the case that this voice guide approach is not something you set in stone once. It's kind of an evolving thing.

## One paragraph, two ways

So we tried doing better on one paragraph from [Part 4](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-4-the-window-within/), about Mary, the therapist who gets kidnapped by Clark, one of her patients (spoilers for the film; Async is the research institute exploring the Backrooms). Here it is as published:

> Back to the dinner table, though. [One of the analysis videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=A6adyYDMpD8) I watched afterwards read Mary's moment there, when she tells Clark she can't save anyone, as bravery. I think it's bravery and defeat at once. She's at her lowest point in the film when she realizes she can't save anyone, and that same realization is what frees her. Maybe she looks back on it later and is half grateful for it, even though it came from being kidnapped and tied up by her own patient. I honestly don't know how she goes back to doing therapy after that, though Async probably isn't going to let her go anyway, and my guess is she ends up working for them.

And here is the same paragraph using what I said in the message I dictated about that scene, with the filler cut:

> Back to the dinner table, though. One of the analysis videos I watched afterwards read Mary's moment there, when she tells Clark she can't save anyone, as bravery. Having that kind of realization does require a level of vulnerability, and you could interpret that as bravery if you want, but it doesn't have to be either or. She is at a low point in that scene, where she realizes that she can't save anyone, but that is also her liberation. In retrospect, she might look back on it and be semi thankful for it, even if it involved being kidnapped and tied up by one of her patients. I don't know how she could go back to doing therapy after that. Imagine that trauma coming up again every time she sees another patient. What if she runs into another Clark? I guess it's irrelevant, because Async probably isn't going to let her go. The most likely outcome is that she ends up working for them as a kind of field psychologist, but I'm just speculating at that point.

You can see the swaps in that one paragraph. Where I said "semi thankful", the post said "half grateful". Where I said "liberation", the post said "what frees her". It was like that across the whole series. When I wasn't sure of something, I said "I guess" (60 times across my Backrooms messages), while the posts said "seems to" 17 times and "I guess" once. None of that changes the meaning, but it isn't how I talk.

That second version took two tries. The first attempt kept nearly everything I'd dictated, and it felt more like me, but it was also noticeably less polished. So I guess there's a balance to be figured out there. There is the concept of having a writing voice, which might be different than your spoken voice. A blog is a bit more formal than a DM, but it's less formal than a college essay. I ramble when I dictate (one of my messages for this post ends with "Sorry, I feel like I lost my train of thought a little bit. I've been talking for too long."), and I do ramble over text too, just a bit differently. So the target now is my own words with the rambling cut out.

## Hedges, swearing, and trying too hard

Then there are the hedges, the "I guess" and "I think" that go in front of a claim. If you do it too often, you come off as someone who doesn't know what the fuck they're talking about. If you do it too infrequently, people think you're overly confident or something. Me hedging a lot may also stem from a lack of self-confidence and/or depression, so it's not necessarily a good thing. Claude suggested a test, which is to ask whether the claim is actually uncertain. If I'm guessing what happens to a character after the credits, the hedge stays, because I am just speculating at that point. If it's something I believe, the post can just say it.

We went through the rest of the voice guide the same way (swearing, parentheses, sentence length, vocabulary), and I felt like a broken record. For all of these things, I was like, let's use it if it makes the writing better, rather than let's just use it for the sake of using it. Take swearing. If I'm just rambling on about something, I'm going to drop all the F-bombs and not even realize. That's just how I talk. In writing, a swear word can be an effective intensifier to get a certain point across, but that doesn't work if every other word is an F-bomb. A well-placed swear every now and again is probably fine, as long as it serves the writing and it's not just, oh, haha, I'm dropping F-bombs because I can.

One of the criticisms of the Hellaverse (the adult animated shows [*Hazbin Hotel*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazbin_Hotel) and [*Helluva Boss*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helluva_Boss)) is that there's too much swearing and slapstick and just crude content. But in a weird way, it kind of works, because that's the vibe that the universe is trying to go for. You hear so many swears in those shows that a lot of people complain about them, but they're actually part of why I like the series so much. They just feel refreshingly crude or uncut. I really do think Vivziepop, who created both, is doing an excellent job. And honestly, I respect the shit out of her for just doing what she wants to do, regardless of what the haters say.

As for the parentheses, they're probably stylistically associated with my writing at this point, considering that my old posts from 2013 to 2018 had them (I promised "copious amounts of parenthetical addenda such as this one" in my very first post). So I don't know. It's not something we have to adopt. As for sentence length, we don't need to hit what my old posts used to do, because I was trying too hard. Instead of writing as myself, I was trying to impress by using fancy vocabulary and whatnot. Sometimes I use vocabulary like that even in regular day-to-day speech, and we don't have to ban specific words if I've actually used them, but it should be used when it improves the writing. I know that's a judgment call and a taste call, so that's the hard part.

## What happens to the Backrooms posts

Nothing. We could go back and put my own sentences back into the series, but I'd rather leave the posts as is, do a new post about voice tweaking that discusses this very process (this one), and then begin applying the result to future posts. That will read more as growth, which I think is the more honest approach. This post is the first one written the new way (for what it's worth, Claude ran the same script on it, and it scores 45% with the two quoted paragraphs counted).

Since we're using AI collaboration as our blog post writing model, I think creating a voice guide is an important part of that. I think it will help with maintaining that air of authenticity even with the AI collaboration. And at the end of the day, what we're doing is kind of what you do anyway as a writer. You're trying to figure out how to write.
