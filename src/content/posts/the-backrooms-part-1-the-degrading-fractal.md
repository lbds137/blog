If you've spent much time online in the last few years, you've probably seen the picture: a big, empty room with yellowed wallpaper, damp-looking carpet, and buzzing fluorescent lights, the kind of office space that feels like it's been closed for years. It was posted to 4chan in May 2019, in a thread asking for images that just feel "off", and the next day someone gave it a name and a story. If you're not careful, the story went, you can slip out of reality the way a video game character sometimes falls through a wall (gamers call it noclipping). You end up in [the Backrooms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Backrooms): an endless maze of rooms like that one, with nothing in it but the hum of the lights. The photo, it later turned out, was taken during the renovation of an old furniture store, which matters once we get to the film.

The idea spread fast, but the version most people know now comes from [Kane Parsons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kane_Parsons). He was sixteen when he posted his first Backrooms video to YouTube in January 2022, a short piece of fake camcorder footage he'd built in Blender, a free 3D animation program, and it looked disturbingly real. Over the following videos he built a whole world around the place, including the Async Research Institute (Async for short), which has found a way in and is exploring it, with mixed results.

This year A24 turned it into [a feature film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backrooms_(film)), directed by Parsons himself, and that's where I came in. I'd never actually watched the YouTube series, and I went in mostly knowing the meme. I came out wishing there was more, so I kept going back. I've lost count of how many times I've watched it since, but it's somewhere around eight, which, for a movie about what happens when you keep returning to something, is a little on the nose.

A lot of those viewings turned into long late-night conversations with Claude, Anthropic's AI, and the notes piled up until they made four posts. They aren't a review or a theory of the ending. They're more of a tour of what the film set off in my head, roughly in the order it happened. This first one starts on the night I first saw it and covers everything it reminded me of, and the pattern I found underneath. The second follows that pattern down into what occultists call the Abyss, and into the story of Clark, the man at the center of the film. The third is about the place itself and Async, the research institute watching it, and the fourth is about Mary, his therapist. Spoilers for the whole film from here on.

## The film, briefly

The film is set in 1990, in San Jose, and it starts where the photo did, in a furniture store. Clark's store is failing, and his marriage is failing along with it: his wife, Barbara, has thrown him out, and he's been sleeping in the store. The lights keep acting up, and when a meter man from the power company comes to look at the basement breaker box, they find a set of breakers sitting at an odd angle that don't seem to connect to anything. Then Clark finds an opening in the basement wall that shouldn't be there, and it leads into the Backrooms.

He gets obsessed, especially with a steep ramp down to a dark lower level that he can't explore alone. So he talks Kat, his assistant manager, and her boyfriend Bobby into coming in to film it, promising overtime and saying only that it's for "research", however hard Kat presses. It may be the first time Clark doesn't respect someone's consent: agreeing for money to something you haven't been told about isn't really agreeing. Then again, under capitalism most people only work because they have to, so a lot of agreeing for money isn't quite agreeing either. Bobby has to come anyway, since the camcorder is on loan from his school, though once inside he seems pretty excited to be filming.

Kat's first theory is that Clark has dosed them, and after the way he's kept them in the dark, it's not a crazy guess. Bobby says he'd know if he were high. "But you are," she says, and he says, "Pot doesn't count." I felt so called out in that scene. Later, they lower Bobby down the ramp on a rope, and when he glimpses something down there he scrambles back up, rattled. He can't undo Clark's knot, and he's asking in frustration what kind of knot it even is when something yanks the rope from below. Kat and Clark can't hold him, and the pull is so hard that the heavy bed the rope is tied to slides forward and shoves them down the ramp after him. By the end, Bobby is dead and the two of them have been separated.

The other person who ends up down there is Clark's therapist, Mary Kline, and she's carrying her own past. Her mother, Nora, seems to have been agoraphobic (the film shows more than it tells): young Mary wasn't allowed outside, or even to open the windows, and Nora was eventually institutionalized. Mary has since written a self-help book called *The Window Within*. When Clark leaves her a message saying he's opened the window and won't be coming back, she goes looking for him and follows him in.

Inside, the Backrooms copies things. Rooms from the real world turn up in there, almost right, and so do people: slow, hollow copies the film calls still lifes, and one far worse one, a pirate version of Clark based on the mascot from his store's TV commercials, which turns out to be what dragged Bobby away. Async is watching through cameras in at least a couple of the rooms. Much of the last third of the film takes place at a dinner table Clark has set up down there. When Mary finds Clark and asks what this place is, his answer is the closest the film comes to explaining itself: it's every place that ever was.

## Things it reminded me of

I watched it for the first time late at night, and by the time it was over my brain was already reaching for comparisons. The first one was [*The Stanley Parable*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Stanley_Parable), a 2013 video game in which you play an office worker named Stanley whose coworkers have all vanished, while a narrator describes what you're doing and gets irritated when you don't do it. Both have the same not-quite-right office building, and both play with what's called non-Euclidean space, rooms that couldn't physically fit inside the rooms around them. The difference is tone. *The Stanley Parable* is a comedy at heart, a weird piece of meta-commentary mostly about choice and control in video games, though I've never been entirely sure what it's trying to say. *The Backrooms* plays the same kind of space completely straight.

There was something else nagging at me too, and I couldn't place it; at the time I said it would either come back to me or be lost forever in the Backrooms, which felt appropriate. Claude guessed [SCP-3008](https://scp-wiki.wikidot.com/scp-3008), an IKEA that may go on forever, from the collaborative horror wiki SCP, which I'd heard of but never dug into; not the one, but a good guess for a film about a furniture store.

While I was trying to remember, I got a couple of near misses. One was a horror game I'd watched [someone play on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6yp2OGKVQ1g). I couldn't remember its name (my best guess was something like "Bobby's Burger Palace", probably because I once ate at the real one, Bobby Flay's burger chain, in DC), and Claude worked out that it was [*Happy's Humble Burger Farm*](https://www.dreadcentral.com/news/485143/happys-humble-burger-farm-serving-up-scares-in-december/), from 2021. You work the night shift at a fast-food restaurant in a grim city and slowly realize that the restaurant, the city, and maybe even you are part of some corporation's experiment (its sequel describes the whole thing as [a reality-warping simulation program](https://www.bluesnews.com/s/299215/happy_s-humble-burgatory-announced-get-happy_s-humble-burger-farm-for-free)). It had a similar vibe, but its strangeness felt built wrong on purpose rather than copied wrong.

The other near miss came from one particular scene. Mary ends up in a squarish room with a bottomless pit in the middle, and the floors below are cut away, so that you can look straight down through level after level of rooms. That reminded me of the end of [*Interstellar*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_(film)), where the astronaut falls into a black hole and ends up in a kind of tesseract, a space where his daughter's bedroom repeats across time and he can reach into it from behind. The same room also reminded me of a Minecraft chunk error, when the game fails to load a piece of the world and leaves a hole straight down through it, so that you can see every layer at once.

Then something in one of Claude's replies jogged it, and the thing that had been nagging at me finally came back: the episode of the animated sci-fi comedy [*Rick and Morty*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_and_Morty) with the decoy families, which Claude knew was called "[Mortyplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mortyplicity)". Rick has made decoy copies of the family to draw off assassins, and the decoys have made decoys of their own, and those have made more, so that a few generations down you end up with very strange, very wrong versions of everyone. That's the kind of horror the Backrooms runs on: copies of copies, each one a little further from the original.

Copies of copies also made me think of a hall of mirrors, or really a funhouse, where every mirror hands you back a twisted version of yourself. The film has its own version of that: walls that only work one way. In one scene Clark is looking for Kat, and she's on the other side of what looks to him like a solid wall, while from her side it's more like a one-way mirror. He puts the camera down, so from its point of view we watch him talk to the wall, while Kat, to her horror, can see what's behind him. Then something picks the camera up, Kat tells Clark to look behind him, and the footage cuts out. That one had my hair raised. It was clever and terrifying at the same time.

After that first night, more kept coming over the following days and weeks. The morning after, it was [*Alice in Wonderland*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice%27s_Adventures_in_Wonderland): there's a room in the film where a hallway seems to narrow into the distance but is actually a steep climb up to a strange little door, with a throne-like chair at the wrong size, and the whole thing has that dreamlike (or nightmarish) feel, except that nobody in the Backrooms talks to you. On a rewatch I noticed that the chair looks identical to the throne that broke under Clark while he was filming his pirate commercial. Down there it's giant, and Claude's way of putting it was that the place keeps his embarrassment at the size it felt.

Later it was *Narnia*, with its hidden door into a world that plays by different rules. Claude added the part I'd missed: the way into [Narnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chronicles_of_Narnia) is a door hidden in a piece of furniture, and Clark runs a furniture store. Claude's other point stuck with me too. Narnia has an author, Aslan, the lion who sings it into being, and the Backrooms is the wardrobe with no Aslan.

Then there was the artist M.C. Escher. I only had the name, and Claude pointed me to his lithograph [*Relativity*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativity_(M._C._Escher)), which is full of staircases where one person's wall is another person's floor. The dark lower level where Bobby is taken is like that, a basement turned on its side, and at one point Bobby looks up at a hallway whose floor is standing straight up. When Kat and Clark get separated down there, Clark crawls through a hole in the ceiling tiles and along a cramped passage, which reminded me a little of the maintenance areas behind the test chambers in the game [*Portal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portal_(video_game)), one of Claude's first-night guesses.

A few of the comparisons were more obscure. [*The Langoliers*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Langoliers_(miniseries)) came back to me later. It's a Stephen King story, made into a TV miniseries in 1995, in which a plane goes through a rip in time into the recent past. There the food tastes of nothing, the drinks are flat, matches won't light, and even voices don't echo, because the past is a dead copy of the present.

Claude pointed out that the Langoliers themselves, the creatures that chew up the past behind the moving present, work like a garbage collector, the part of a program that clears away memory nobody needs anymore. The Backrooms is what you get when there's no garbage collector, so the stale copies just pile up forever. I'd add that getting in there is like a program dereferencing a null pointer, or a buffer overflow, where the program ends up reaching into memory outside the space it's supposed to stay in.

In the animated fantasy series [*She-Ra and the Princesses of Power*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/She-Ra_and_the_Princesses_of_Power) there's Beast Island, an island used as a place of exile and a dumping ground for ancient technology, with grasping, tentacle-like vines, and it has a similar degraded feeling. None of these is a source for the film, as far as I know. My brain just reached for them, and I think the reaching is the point: the Backrooms is built out of half-remembered places, and watching it makes you half-remember them too. That's what sent me looking for the pattern underneath all of them.

## The pattern underneath: a degrading fractal

The image I kept coming back to, starting on that first night, was fractals. A fractal is a shape that contains smaller copies of itself, so that as you zoom in, the same pattern keeps appearing forever; a fern leaf is a natural example. I've loved them for years, and a real fractal is mathematically sound. Every part matches every other part, consistently, and nothing gets lost along the way. The Backrooms is a degrading fractal instead. The more you zoom in, the worse it gets, and it isn't even a fixed picture you could study, because it keeps getting more distorted the longer it goes on.

The film shows this quite literally. Just before the dinner-table scene, the camera goes down through the house Mary grew up in, then through copies of it, each one more stripped out than the last. On my third watch I counted eight levels. The last one is an empty room with a dark archway, and Clark's voice is coming from somewhere through it, just before Mary wakes up at his table. Eight is also about how many times I've watched the film, which I didn't notice until I was reading this over.

All this copying sent me back to Plato too, because the Backrooms is his idea in reverse. He thought the things we see are imperfect copies of perfect originals, which he called [Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_forms), so that every chair you've ever sat in is a copy of the idea of a chair. The Backrooms is what you get if you keep going in the wrong direction: you describe something you saw, then describe your description, and so on, the telephone game of degradation.

Lots of people besides Kane Parsons make Backrooms videos, and in one of them, from a series called [*No Escape*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9DI6bJf03ko&list=PL6T85gHCyjn24csyvRDM14wzssj602kt0) by a channel called Complex Research, the cameraman walks through what looks like an art gallery where one room has the same two paintings repeated over and over. That made me laugh, because Claude had just told me that Plato didn't think much of painting: to him a painting was a copy of a copy.

There's a real-world version of this, and it isn't funny at all. When people or animals are bred too closely, genetic errors pile up instead of washing out. The Habsburgs, the royal family that ruled much of Europe for centuries, married their own cousins and nieces so often that the "Habsburg jaw" became a family trait. [A 2019 study](https://www.sci.news/biology/habsburg-jaw-inbreeding-07866.html) tied how severe it was to how inbred each of them was, and the worst case, Charles II of Spain, died in 1700 without children. [Pugs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pug) are the same story in dogs, bred for flat faces for so long that many of them struggle to breathe. Nobody chose those outcomes. It's just what copying without a fresh source does over enough generations.

And then there's memory, which is where it got personal for me. My memory for facts is pretty good, but getting specific events back out on demand is harder, and years of using cannabis probably haven't helped (though it could just as easily be age). The effect is mild with any one use, but it seems to build up over time, the same way the Backrooms wears down the people who stay in it. And here was a film about a place that remembers badly. Claude pointed out that human memory really does work a bit like the Backrooms. Every time you recall something, your brain stores it again, so you never get back the original, only your last copy of it, and the memories you go back to most are the ones that have been edited most.

The irony landed while I was working on all of this. Early on, Claude made a list of the film's key scenes that ended with a pan down through the layers, and I went along with it. I was sure the ending panned down through layer after layer of degraded rooms, and I kept saying so, until a rewatch showed I'd misremembered: the only pan down is the one before the dinner table, and the ending is a montage of separate shots. I had done to the film exactly what the Backrooms does to everything it touches.

Next time I'll get to where all of this led me, which was down: into the Abyss of Western occultism, and into Clark. After that, the place itself and Async, and then Mary.
