Last time, in [Part 1](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-1-the-degrading-fractal/), I went through everything the new *Backrooms* film set off in my head, starting the night I first saw it, from *The Stanley Parable* to the Habsburgs, and the pattern I found under all of it: a degrading fractal, a world made of copies of copies that get worse the further down you go. As before, a lot of this came out of long conversations with Claude, and where a specific idea was Claude's rather than mine, I say so where it comes up. Spoilers for the whole film again.

If you haven't read that post or seen the film, Part 1 has a short summary. The main things to know are Clark, the failing furniture store owner who finds a way in under his store; Kat, his assistant manager, and her boyfriend Bobby, who go in with him; Mary, his therapist, who follows him in; the slow, hollow copies of people the film calls still lifes, and the much worse pirate copy of Clark; and Async, the research institute watching all of it. You may be wondering where a pattern like that leads. For me, it led down.

## Where it led: the Abyss

The more I think about the Backrooms, the more confusing it gets, which I guess says something about my own understanding degrading too. It isn't something you can solve with logic, and every character who goes in says some version of the same thing first: this doesn't make sense. That feeling of falling into something that can't be understood is what sent me, still on that first night, to the Abyss.

The Abyss means a couple of things to me. There's [*Made in Abyss*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Made_in_Abyss), an anime about a giant pit in the earth where each layer down is more dangerous than the last, and where climbing back up brings on a curse that gets worse the deeper you've been. But mostly I mean the Abyss in Western occultism.

[Kabbalah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabbalah), the Jewish mystical tradition that a lot of Western occultism borrowed from, maps reality as the [Tree of Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_of_life_(Kabbalah)), a diagram of ten spheres connected by paths. It even has a name for a degrading fractal, which Claude pointed out to me. The Tree repeats itself perfectly at every scale, and its shadow, the [Qlipot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qliphoth) (the word means "shells" or "husks"), is the same pattern repeated with loss: empty forms that keep the shape of the spheres but not the light inside them.

That same Tree is where the Abyss comes in. In the occult tradition that grew up around [Aleister Crowley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleister_Crowley), the English occultist of the early twentieth century, the Abyss is the gulf that separates the top three spheres of the Tree from the other seven, and crossing it is the great ordeal of a magician's path, the point where you have to let go of your ego to reach real understanding.

In the middle of the Abyss sits [Da'at](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Da%27at), Hebrew for "knowledge", a hidden sphere that isn't counted among the ten, and crossing the Abyss means passing through it. (I have it tattooed on my throat, which is where it sits when the Tree is mapped onto the body.) In Kenneth Grant's later take on Crowley's system, it's also the gateway to the Qlipot, the shadow tree. Claude connected that to the opening in Clark's basement wall, a door that was never supposed to be there, leading out of the world and into its degraded copy.

Waiting in the Abyss is [Choronzon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choronzon), a demon who first turns up in the sixteenth-century writings of [John Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee) and [Edward Kelley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Kelley), two Elizabethan occultists, and whom Crowley called the Dweller in the Abyss. Crowley's own record of meeting it puts it in three words, "Choronzon is Dispersion", and adds that it "cannot fix his mind upon any one thing for any length of time." When he and his student [Victor Neuburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Neuburg) called it up in the Algerian desert in 1909, his account has it changing shape again and again as it tried to break into Neuburg's protective circle (Claude found that account, and it fits a little too well). Fall into it and you don't come out whole.

Seen that way, the Backrooms is basically the Abyss, and the degraded copies you meet in there, some of which are versions of yourself, make a pretty good picture of Choronzon. They're there to keep you from reaching some kind of greater understanding. The whole place is a trap.

That first night I also wondered whether your odds in there depend on the state of your mind going in. The place wears you down the longer you stay, so if you're already carrying something unresolved, things probably fall apart faster. Clark and Mary are both carrying plenty. I even asked Claude what would happen if Jesus wandered in on his second coming. Would he see degraded crosses everywhere? Would even someone in perfect mental health lose it, just more slowly? I keep thinking about it.

Clark fails that test about as clearly as anyone could. Later in the film, at the dinner table where he ends up holding Mary captive, he tells her that he thinks the copies are better than the originals, because they don't feel anything. To prove it, he sticks a knife in the neck of one of them, a bearded man whose face has been copied over itself until he has six eyes, and the man doesn't react. Clark envies that. [Georgia Dow](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-x7nmr01ZR0), a therapist who analyzes films on YouTube, points out that his own feelings are painful enough that he drinks to get away from them, and that down there he's surrounded himself with people who can't leave him and can't be hurt by him. That's what failing the Abyss looks like: instead of crossing, you come apart.

Except the man isn't as empty as Clark thinks. I didn't catch it myself. Months after my first watch, someone [posted a clip on Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/backroomsfilm/comments/1wxqkuo/i_never_noticed_that_he_blinks/) showing that with the knife still in his neck he blinks, and his three pairs of eyes blink out of step with each other. Blinking is involuntary, so something in there is still running. Clark's proof that the copies feel nothing sits in the same shot as the evidence against it, and the film never points it out, which to me makes it one of the most horrifying details in the whole thing.

Those eyes blinking out of step with each other also tie into one of my favorite threads from those conversations, though it started long before the clip. On the first night I'd already thought the research institute's name fit: something synchronous happens in step with something else, and something asynchronous is out of step, arriving whenever it arrives. On one rewatch I noticed that Clark shows up late to the therapy session where he first tells Mary about the Backrooms and blames the traffic, and it looks as if he's already slipping out of sync with the rest of the world. The blinking came up much later, and it was Claude, not me, who tied it back to the name: the thing at the table literally blinks out of sync. It isn't the strangest thing in the film that runs out of step, either, but that one can wait for Part 3.

## The rewatches: Clark

Those were mostly ideas. It was on the rewatches that the characters came into focus for me, and with them the moment I now think of as the film's turning point, when Clark takes Mary. I didn't see it coming at all the first time.

She finds him in the Backrooms right after coming across a wall covered in his drawings and writing (more on that in Part 3). He's been hiding and watching her, and when she asks him why, he says she's being too loud. When she asks how long he's been down there, he hesitates, as if he's lost track. Going by the mail piled up under the store's door when she went in, it's been days at least, maybe weeks. He tells her she found it, and when she asks what this place is, he says it's every place that ever was.

Then comes heavy thumping from something heading their way, probably drawn by their voices, and he says, "Stay calm. It's only me." So by then he's already met the pirate copy of himself, and I half wonder whether they're working together. Near the end of the film, Phil, the Async scientist who interviews her, shows Mary a badly redacted photo of Clark facing the pirate, as if they're talking, taken by a camera we never see. Back in the Backrooms, Clark grabs her and cuts off her air until she passes out, telling her as he does it that it's for her own good. How is that for her own good? That's when you understand how far gone he is.

When she wakes up, she's tied to a chair at his dinner table, and he's built himself a household down there. The red-haired woman in a red suit standing behind him plays the part of his wife, Barbara. I used to take her for a copy of Barbara, but the consensus online is that she's a stand-in for Barbara rather than Barbara herself.

She's the same figure that came after him earlier in a room decorated for Christmas. That was earlier in the film, just after the pirate dragged Bobby away, when Clark had lost Kat and was still running from it. A man in a wheelchair was there too, turning on his lamp as Clark walked past the tree. So Clark must have gone back there at some point, and it seems he made friends with them. We don't know how long he's been down there, but he's been putting down roots.

When he asks the woman in red to get the lights, she doesn't do it. Georgia Dow caught something I'd missed every time: she lowers her head, as if she's ashamed she couldn't help. So Clark gets up to do it himself, the man in the wheelchair turns on his lamp for some mood lighting, and Clark thanks him.

In the fridge he keeps Kat's head, and he introduces it by her job title: "That's my assistant manager." He says something about having wanted to help her and her refusing, and on one rewatch I started to suspect he killed her himself, though the film never says so. In his director's commentary, as [a breakdown of it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GDnjrXEpaFY) by greg_nog reports, Parsons says Clark kept it on purpose, hoping the place would remember her and bring her back. Claude's read is that he's holding on to what he couldn't save, and pinning his hopes on a copy.

Then Clark makes Mary role-play his wife, the way they used to do in therapy, with hair he's cut, scalp and all, from the woman in red as her wig. On one rewatch I realized it's the earlier session turned inside out. In her office he was apologetic and on the back foot, while Mary, playing Barbara, pushed him with sharper questions so he could work through what he felt. When she asked if he wanted to forge a new path, he said, "Sure, why not?", but as Georgia Dow points out, he was shaking his head. Words are easy, she says, and action is hard. At the table, when he says he went for a few beers after work and Barbara's line is to ask how many, he doesn't hedge: as many as he wanted, because it's his house.

That line has a history. The night his marriage ended, he came home late after drinking, broke a glass, woke Barbara up, and she threw him out. Here, in a distorted copy of a house, with a stand-in for her standing behind him, he gets to replay that night and win it. Some people online think the house is a copy of his own. They point to what may be a photo of him and Barbara on the wall, and to a corner of the room with an odd, sharp angle, a bit like his office at the store. I can't confirm the photo, but it would fit. Another sore spot in that role-play is children: Barbara seems to want them, and Clark's answer, every time, is that they cost money.

What breaks the spell is Mary refusing to play along. She drops the role and tells him the truth, that his problem is that he never takes responsibility and always has something else to blame, including his own brain, and for a moment it actually gets through to him.

## The loop and the way out

Loops are all over this film. Mary's book is about people getting stuck in the same patterns, the well-worn paths their minds keep taking. At one point a voice-over of her talking about loops plays over a shot of a ship's wheel, which made me think of [samsara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sa%E1%B9%83s%C4%81ra), the cycle of rebirth in Hindu and Buddhist thought that you keep going around until something breaks it.

There's the [ouroboros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouroboros) too, the snake eating its own tail, and at the dinner table Clark pulls the stuffing out of one of the still lifes by hand and serves it up as food. People say you are what you eat, so what does a diet of degraded copies make you? Mary doesn't eat it, and Claude pointed out the old rule from myths about the underworld, the one [Persephone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persephone) broke: eat the food down there and you can't leave.

I think of the moment Mary pushes back in programming terms. A loop is a piece of code that repeats, and it needs an exit condition, something that tells it when to stop, or it runs forever. Everything else in the Backrooms is a copy, and copies don't push back, so Clark could have run his loop down there indefinitely. Mary is the loop's exit condition, the one thing in that room that can say no. And it almost works. He asks her how he can fix it, and she says she has no idea, that it's not up to her, and that she can't save anyone. She's given up, and he decides he doesn't want to change after all, because he likes it down there.

Mary's answer is the one clean bit of consent in the film: "Then stay. Right where you are. But let me go." And he does. He starts to untie her, and right then the woman in red starts slamming herself into the corner of the room. At first I thought she was summoning something, but what I read online suggested she senses what's coming and is trying to get away from the door, and my theory is that Clark untying Mary is what brings it.

What comes is the pirate copy of Clark, and it bites him, and I don't think the timing is an accident. Change is the one thing the Backrooms can't do. It can only copy, and every copy is a little worse than the last, so when Clark says out loud that he won't change, he stops being someone passing through and becomes part of the place. Someone on Reddit matched the bite to Goya's painting [*Saturn Devouring His Son*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn_Devouring_His_Son), and I can't unsee it now.

Georgia Dow reads the pirate as Clark's shadow, and the bite as Clark finally giving it permission to keep being what it is, so that it consumes him the way he consumed the people around him. Even so, I don't think the pirate is malicious. None of the copies are, as far as I can tell. They're just deprived of their minds, and the pirate is the hungriest of them.

One last thing about Clark. I think he's a more relatable character than he first looks, and not just to me. A lot of people find adult life disappointing. Not everyone, but many of us give up on our dreams at some point in exchange for a more practical life. Clark wanted to be an architect, and instead he's running a furniture store that's failing, separated from his wife and sleeping on the display beds. So when a man who wanted to design buildings finds an endless building under his own store (that way of putting it is Claude's), it's not hard to see why he can't stay away from it.

I see a little of myself in him too, not much, but some. I know what it is to reach for something to take the edge off a bad feeling. The difference is what we do with it: he hurts people before they can hurt him (that's Georgia Dow's point, and I think she's right), while I tend to withdraw and become my own problem instead of anyone else's. Neither is great, but his is a lot more dangerous to the people around him. Mary has her own version too. At a gathering at her house, she sees a mother and daughter who seem much closer than she and Nora were, slips into another room, and takes pills from an unlabeled ziplock bag in a cabinet, a foil to his drinking.

That's Clark's side of it. Next time: the place itself, and Async, the research institute that found it before he did.
