This is the third of four posts about the *Backrooms* film. [Part 1](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-1-the-degrading-fractal/) was the first night: everything it reminded me of, and the degrading fractal underneath. [Part 2](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-2-the-one-thing-it-cant-do/) followed that pattern down into the Abyss and through Clark's story, up to the moment the pirate copy of him takes him. This one is about the place itself, and about Async, the research institute that was down there before any of them. The place doesn't keep time in order, and neither does this post: I'm going by what each thing explains, not when it happens in the film. Same as before: the ideas are mine unless I say they're Claude's, and there are spoilers for the whole film.

If you're starting here, the short version: Clark runs a failing furniture store in 1990 and finds a way into the Backrooms under it. He takes Kat, his assistant manager, and her boyfriend Bobby in with him, and Bobby gets killed down there. Mary, Clark's therapist, follows him in later. The place is full of still lifes, slow, hollow copies of people, and one far worse copy, a pirate version of Clark based on the mascot from his store's TV commercials. Phil is the Async scientist who interviews Mary at the end.

## Every place that ever was

When Mary asks Clark where they are, he tells her it's "every place that ever was", and it does seem to run on rules. The clearest is one Claude put into words: sound is the one thing that gets through. Kat can hear Clark through a wall that only works one way, music plays out of the walls, and wind chimes keep turning up.

There are chimes in the Async footage at the start of the film, just before the pirate attacks their cameraman (I'll get to that footage). Clark has them hanging in his basement, where there's no wind to ring them, which Claude read as a kind of alarm for the opening in the wall. Running from the pirate, Mary bumps her head on some that hang a little too low for her, right before the room where she hurts her foot (Part 4 tells that run), and they're also in her childhood home with her mentally ill mother. Then there are chimes again in the interview room at the end.

The copies seem to have a rule of their own, and it's in their name. A still life is a painting of objects with no living subject, and as the YouTuber [greg_nog points out](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tFCUSbY3eC0), it's also a term from Conway's Game of Life, a simple simulation where grids of cells change by fixed rules. There, a still life is a pattern the rules leave unchanged forever, and the film's costume designer put the Game of Life on Phil's shirt. That's the copies exactly: things that will never change again.

Staying is its own danger. The longer you're in there, the more the line between what's real and what's a twisted copy blurs, and I think if you live in a world of corrupted copies long enough, the corruption filters down into you. It almost makes me think of original sin, or, as Claude suggested, the Kabbalistic idea that the world was broken before any of us got here, though maybe I'm reading too much into it.

## The leak

The Backrooms doesn't stay put, either. On the first night I got stuck on a loose end from the start of the film: those odd breakers in Clark's basement, which Clark didn't remember being there. To me they looked like the Backrooms leaking out a little. And copies of Clark's store turn up inside the place, so the leak runs both ways: the Backrooms seeps out, and bits of the world seep in.

A rewatch gave me more. The first time Clark and the meter man from the power company switch the odd breakers off, nothing happens. On another night, though, the power is acting up, Clark flips all the other breakers off with no effect, and the odd ones are what finally cut the power to his store. The lights don't come back on, and he's about to give up and go back upstairs when he sees a tiny sliver of light in the wall, visible only from the perfect angle. With the lights on he'd never have seen it, so the leak is what shows him the way in. It's picking up steam, and taken to its logical conclusion, it's slowly eating the world.

Clark may be carrying some of it out by hand, too. I don't think those basement chimes were there the whole time. The basement starts out as extra space for the furniture showroom and gradually becomes Clark's base of operations, and when he ropes Kat and Bobby into joining him, Bobby remarks on how much stuff is down there. It seems Clark has been exfiltrating more and more things from the Backrooms, so those chimes could be part of the haul, although that's just my theory.

He may even have worked out what was happening. Just before Mary finds him, she comes across a wall that someone, almost certainly Clark, has covered in drawings and writing. It reads like a lost man's research notes. "Tables don't bleed blood" is his test of the still lifes, written in the language of a furniture salesman, and "Why think in terms of magic?? Be REALISTIC!!!" is him arguing with himself. The one that stopped me was "Here and now is a two way street". That's the leak again, the Backrooms and the world seeping into each other, written on a wall by someone who'd worked it out from the inside.

## Async got there first

Clark wasn't the first to start working this place out. Async, or the Async Research Institute, is the outfit studying the Backrooms: the scientists, the video operators, the guys in hazmat suits, and Phil, who ends up across the table from Mary. The film barely tells you what it is. Phil says near the end that it used to make MRI machines, and a framed clipping in his kitchen, dated June 1987, says it was a Bay Area imaging startup that won a $1.2 million federal grant for next-generation MRI hardware.

In the [YouTube series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backrooms_(web_series)) Kane Parsons made before he directed the film, Async doesn't just find the place. It opens the way in itself. The clipping says the grant would pay for tests to reduce field distortions in MRI hardware, and about a year later, Async starts on what it calls a low-proximity magnetic distortion system. Its sixth test, on October 17, 1989, opens a gateway into the Backrooms, and in the series' story it also sets off the Loma Prieta earthquake, the real one that hit the Bay Area that day. So it may have opened the door while trying to fix a flaw in its scanners. The film never spells any of that out, but that backstory explains why Async was down there long before Clark.

## The tape that was there first

The film itself opens on Async's footage, dated June 19, 1990. Hold on to that date. Async's video operator, Naren Warne, is running through the Backrooms in a panic with his camera, separated from his team. He finds wires leading back to Async's equipment and follows them, relieved, through a gap in the wall that he blocks behind him with a heavy wheeled case. But the radio in the room beyond doesn't work, and nobody answers.

Then he hears a horrible shriek. He heads toward it, and in the film's first jump scare, a seagull smacks into a wall in a burst of feathers and drops as if it's dead, until it suddenly gets up. Looking for where it came from, he finds a patch of wet ceiling tile with feathers and leaves on the ground below it, and you can faintly hear birds in the distance. My guess is that the bird found a small way in and wandered through. The film keeps coming back to birds. Later on, Clark sees a dead bird in the Backrooms and moves on. It may be the same one, though that's unconfirmed.

Warne doesn't get long to wonder about the bird. A door opens down the hallway behind him, the way he came, and when he sees the pirate copy of Clark partly emerging from it, he breaks into a panicked run. He makes a wrong turn into a room with a beach and palm trees on the wallpaper and a locked door. When he goes to peek out the door he came in through, the pirate suddenly hits him, he falls to the ground, and the camera drops as he screams. When the footage ends the screen goes dark, and you can see two or three Async researchers reflected in it, so the tape made it back to them, even if Warne almost certainly didn't.

"Everything Must Go", about fifteen minutes of extra footage that theaters later started adding after the credits, goes back to before that tape. The copy I'd bought on YouTube didn't have it, so for a while I could only see it in a bootleg someone posted, until Kane Parsons put it up on his channel as [a separate video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ewZx0bnBb30).

It shows how Warne's team reached that part of the Backrooms. They find closing-sale signs from Clark's store, each one smaller than the last, and being scientists, they measure everything. The tiny imperfections line up perfectly from sign to sign at each scale, so they're all one sign, copied. Since the sizes keep shrinking toward the back wall, they guess the pattern continues behind it and cut through to check, and they're right. In the space between the walls, Warne (I think) sees a hand sticking out, which turns out to belong to a mannequin on the other side, next to a ship's wheel. The room beyond is dark, unlike most of what we've seen of the Backrooms, and I can't help wondering if that connects to the odd breakers.

Further in is a smaller, emptier copy of the store, with a TV that switches back and forth between a blue screen (with what looked to me like Chinese characters) and someone's home video, never quite in rhythm. Warne is the one who keeps wanting to leave and come back with more people, and the others keep overruling him. The short also explains why he's alone at the start of the film: the team runs into the pirate, he runs away, and he gets separated from the others, who seem to have survived. So the one who wanted to turn back is probably the only one who dies.

In Part 2 I said the blinking eyes weren't the strangest thing in the film to run out of step. The strangest is that opening tape. On a rewatch I noticed that it already has pieces of Clark's store in it: the "No Credit, No Problem" banner sticking out of a ceiling, and the ship's-wheel clock from Clark's office. Clark doesn't go in for the first time until June 29. The copy of Clark was in there ten days before Clark was. It's the same out-of-step pattern as the blinking eyes and Clark showing up late to therapy, only bigger, and about as asynchronous as it gets.

Eventually, Async works out whose copy that is. It happens at home, possibly the only time we see Phil outside work, as a regular dude with his family. They're watching a movie about aliens, and he gets up to make popcorn, which is when we see that clipping in his kitchen. We don't see his face in the scene until one of Clark's commercials comes on, and I think that's the moment he realizes the man from their footage is a furniture salesman from San Jose. The commercial's slogan is "Get lost in an endless sea", and once I noticed it, the sea was everywhere: the pirate costume, that ship's-wheel clock, even the TV ad for the book Mary wrote, which shows a man on a ship at sea. Claude's way of putting it was that the place is running Clark's own ad copy back at him.

## The recording and the bait

Async is doing more than watching, too. At one point the Backrooms' loudspeakers play a recording of greetings in 55 languages, some of them long dead, and I first took it for the place resurrecting old languages. It's actually the kind of message people send into space hoping to make contact. When I described it to Claude, 55 languages and a line about "the children of planet Earth", Claude matched it to the greetings NASA put on the [Voyager Golden Record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyager_Golden_Record) in 1977. The film shows a tape spinning while it plays, with Phil watching the feed.

Watch this part with the captions on. They name the languages as they go by, including ancient ones like Akkadian, Hittite, Aramaic, and Sumerian, which you'd never pick out by ear. The record has a greeting in Hebrew too, and Claude pointed out that it's the one language on the record that stopped being anyone's everyday speech for centuries and then came back. In a place where nothing that's lost ever returns, only copies of it, that's an odd thing to be playing.

My guess is that the recording is bait, played to draw the pirate out where their cameras can see it. Sound gets through, after all. The recording plays from a cardboard caveman cutout with a wire running into its back, so someone has rigged it as a speaker, and there seems to be an Async camera pointed at it.

Clark gets a glimpse of this from the other end. One night at the store, while he's watching an alien movie on the TV by the display bed he sleeps in, the picture switches for a few moments to what looks like an Async camera feed before the screen goes dark. Later, in the Backrooms, he follows the recording to the room where it's playing, and behind a hidden panel there, given away by a scrap of fabric sticking out, he finds Warne's bag and badge.

The bait seems to work. From there, Clark reaches the room with the giant throne from Part 1, a blown-up copy of the one that broke under him while he was filming his pirate commercial. What look like Bobby's and Kat's shoes are sunk into its floor. (Bobby had already found another copied piece of himself in the dark lower level where he gets killed: in a pile of laundry, a copy of the End Apartheid shirt he was wearing the day the original throne broke. He quietly swears, so maybe it creeps him out too.) Then there's a noise from the room Clark just left, and the pirate destroys the cutout that was playing the tape. I doubt that's a coincidence.

That cutout probably wasn't the first to go. Earlier in the film, in the dark lower level, there's what looks like the head of another one, probably torn off. It wasn't the last, either: near the end, another one, in the copy of Clark's store that Mary runs through, is ringed with gas canisters and wired to a trap. Part 4 gets to what Async finally catches.

## What Async wants

Async is stuck in a loop of its own. Phil keeps saying they just want to know a little bit more, and I don't think he's as detached as he looks. The whole institute seems to be addicted to exploring the place, and since the place has no end, the exploring never will either, so the institute just goes around and around. You can see it in Warne's team, pushing on past a hand in the wall because, as one of them says, "This is worth doing."

It's hard not to read that as a picture of capitalism too, whether Parsons meant it or not: an organization that always needs a little bit more, chasing growth into a space that will never run out. [One analysis video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CKlOJYBL8_A) I watched made a related point about Clark's furniture store, which is capitalism's copy of a home, built only to get you to buy something. Thrown out of his real home by his wife, Clark is living somewhere Backrooms-like before he ever goes in.

I keep coming back to that 1987 clipping. You could read Async as a company that started out helping people and got tempted away, and it could easily be a fictional version of OpenAI, a nonprofit with lofty goals that turned into a for-profit company (the internet sarcastically calls it ClosedAI). The clipping even names Async's founder, a physicist called Lawrence Altman, which can't help sounding like Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, and [according to the fan wiki](https://kane-pixels-backrooms.fandom.com/wiki/Lawrence_Altman), Kane Parsons once wondered if that name had become too on the nose. Then again, you can make hospital equipment and just be in it for the money, so I don't know how far Async actually fell.

Either way, in the YouTube series it wants more than research early on. Its vice director is Ivan Beck, a figure the series keeps mentioning and never really explains (I've watched it twice and still can't tell you what his deal is). He [pitches the place](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tvmEc7JhR0E) as "a solution to all current and future storage and residential needs" that will "save billions of dollars on property construction and management", and [a corporate video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ITuGdHxHi0A) shows plans for offices, homes, and even trains in there. That's the same point at full scale: a company looks at a place that only copies and sees real estate.

Async's need to keep exploring is the part I recognize in myself, though, and it's part of why the film got its hooks into me. I'm not physically in the Backrooms, but for a while my brain was lodged in there. When something grabs me I have to turn it over and look at every angle, and the Backrooms is built to feed exactly that, because it's infinite and recursive and there's always more of it.

That's the place, and the people watching it. Next time, the last one: [Mary, and how she gets out](/blog/posts/the-backrooms-part-4-the-window-within/).
